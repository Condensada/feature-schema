SCHEMA Digest handbook

Editorial guide for selecting, analyzing, and submitting Digest articles.

Purpose

SCHEMA Digest is the publication's art criticism and journalism corner. It offers critical reviews and analysis of literature such as novels, fiction, self-help books, films, and television or streaming series. A Digest should turn a work into an accessible, in-depth overview: structured, sharp, detailed, and compact. It should develop writers' critical thinking and connect meaningfully with the SAMCIS community.

What makes a strong Digest

  • Technical strength: Organize the article clearly and write it well.
  • Symbolic depth: Explore meanings beyond the most obvious reading.
  • Original insight: Offer a thoughtful interpretation that invites curiosity rather than repeating familiar opinions.
  • Balanced evaluation: Support your conclusions with specific observations while making your own perspective and overall impression clear.

Summarize without spoiling

Write for readers who may not have read or watched the source. Briefly introduce the premise, pivotal characters, relationships, and a few important turning points, but do not reveal the entire story. Choose details carefully to build interest and leave the central discoveries intact.

Analyze the work

Identify what the source is trying to express and examine relevant dimensions such as social, economic, cultural, psychological, emotional, spiritual, behavioral, or philosophical themes. Explain whether the work achieves its main idea, support your evaluation with specific details, and include your own considered response.

Style and use of source material

  • Use clear, accessible prose. Distinctive vocabulary is welcome in moderation; do not make readers work through dense wording.
  • Use a clever, fitting title and subheadings to guide the reader and add voice.
  • Keep quotations and other borrowed material limited. Cite memorable lines accurately, and make sure the article's analysis and original writing remain its focus.

Choosing a source

A source may be a novel, fiction story, self-help book, motion picture, or television/streaming series. Choose work that:

  • Has a clear structure and enough substance for technical and symbolic analysis.
  • Connects to the assigned theme.
  • Offers distinctive or innovative ideas and room for an original interpretation.
  • Engages with relevant social or political questions and supports thoughtful discussion.

Review platforms can help with background research and gauging reception. The source material itself should remain the basis of the article's analysis.

Pitch and approval process

  1. Develop a list of 10 possible sources and assess each against the selection criteria.
  2. Choose your top three and tell the section editor why you recommend them.
  3. The section editor reviews the choices and may challenge whether a source is suitable.
  4. When available, seek Editorial Board Admin approval; otherwise, the section editor makes the final decision.
  5. Discuss and confirm the selected source with the section editor before beginning the article.

Submission and editing

  • Submit the finished article one to two days before its planned posting date to allow time for editorial review and revisions.
  • Upload the article to the designated SCHEMA Google Drive submission folder.
  • Notify the section editor after uploading your work.
  • Follow the editorial workflow and assignment dates in the Planner.

Humans of SAMCIS guide

A practical guide to choosing a subject, conducting an interview, and writing a thoughtful profile.

What the series is

Humans of SAMCIS is a human-interest profile series about the people behind the SAMCIS community: student leaders, organizers, artists, achievers, alumni, and quiet contributors. Each article turns an interview into a story about what drives a person. It is not a résumé or a list of achievements; the subject's voice and a clear human theme should lead.

  • Personal: Let the subject's own words and perspective shape the story.
  • Warm: Be sincere and respectful, without mockery or exaggerated praise.
  • Structured: Build a clear story arc around one central theme.
  • Insightful: Reveal meaning beyond what readers already see.

A profile differs from a SCHEMA Digest: a Digest analyzes a creative work and centers the writer's evaluation; a profile centers the person being interviewed. The writer guides the story but is not its critic.

Choosing a subject

  • Possible subjects include students, student officers, alumni, and non-teaching staff or campus personnel who are part of everyday SAMCIS life.
  • The guide says faculty members are not eligible. Confirm eligibility with the section editor before approaching a subject.
  • Look for a person willing to reflect openly, with values and experiences that can support a meaningful theme such as belonging, burnout, growth, service, or resilience.
  • A title, award, or job alone is not the story. Look for the person behind the role and the effort, choices, or values behind visible achievements.
  • Suggested process: Propose three possible subjects to the section editor, explaining each story's angle and why the community should know it. The editor may ask for a deeper angle and approves the final subject, with an Editorial Board Admin if available.

Choose a format

  • Long-form article: Best for layered stories involving a struggle, change, or lesson. The guide suggests 900–1,300 words, posted as text with subheadings. Suggested title pattern: Humans of SAMCIS: "Name: Tagline."
  • Photo-card feature: Best for a focused theme or portrait of everyday life. The guide suggests 5–7 cards and about 200–300 words total. Use a descriptive cover tagline; place the subject's name on the introduction card. Include the byline, layout credit, and photo credit in the cards.

These lengths and formats are suggestions in the supplied guide; confirm them with the section editor.

Shape the story

Both formats move from first impressions, to something deeper, to a message for the reader.

  • Open: Use a compelling image or a strong quote in the subject's own voice. Give the person and setting, then introduce the central contrast or theme.
  • Go deeper: Show what the role involves beyond its title. Include a concrete scene, visible result, unseen effort, and—if the subject chooses to share it—the cost, doubt, or struggle behind the work.
  • Find the core: Use the subject's reflections to explain what motivates them, what they hope for SAMCIS, and what they have learned.
  • Close: Draw out a lesson for the reader and, where possible, return to an image or idea from the opening.

For photo cards, keep one idea per card, use short text that is easy to read at a glance, emphasize only a key line, and proofread every card before posting.

Style and quote handling

  • Narrate in third person and use the name or nickname the subject prefers.
  • Use a warm, observant, respectful tone. Admire without flattering.
  • Show qualities through specific scenes rather than simply listing traits. Contrast what people assume with what the subject's experience reveals.
  • Mix descriptive sentences with shorter lines. Use creative language with restraint, saving the boldest phrasing for titles, subheadings, and the close.
  • Preserve code-switching (such as Tagalog, Ilocano, and English) exactly as spoken. Translate only when needed to preserve meaning.
  • Quote the subject verbatim; do not polish their grammar or wording. Use bracketed insertions only to clarify context.
  • Frame quotes with narration instead of leaving them unsupported or stacking several together. Keep the writer's interpretation distinct from the subject's words.
  • Choose quotes carefully: a strong quote may open the story, and a candid reflection may anchor its deeper section or ending. Confirm the subject is comfortable with how sensitive quotes are presented.

Plan and conduct the interview

The guide suggests planning a 30–45-minute interview, in person if possible. Beforehand, research the subject's role, verify names, dates, and results, ask how they prefer to be addressed and their pronouns, and get permission to record or take notes. Prepare 10–12 questions, but follow useful tangents.

  • Who they are: How would you describe yourself? What does a regular day look like?
  • Motivation: What do you enjoy most about what you do? What keeps you going on tired days?
  • Vision and meaning: What would you build or change for SAMCIS? What do people misunderstand or overlook about your role?
  • Unseen work: What are you proud of that people may not have seen? Have you felt overwhelmed, and what did you learn?
  • Struggle and growth: Was there a time you felt you did not belong or were not good enough? What happened next?
  • Deeper why: Who or what shaped you? Why do you keep showing up? What helps you stay joyful or kind?
  • Closing: What advice would you give others? What do you want people to know that they usually do not?

Ask follow-up questions such as “why?” and “what was that like?” Give the subject time to think, follow the emotion, and never press them on a topic they decline to discuss.

Ethics, consent, and accuracy

  • Include sensitive disclosures—such as burnout, bullying, family matters, mental health, or religion—only when the subject has chosen to share them. If unsure, ask. Frame vulnerable experiences as growth, not scandal.
  • Ask permission before recording or taking notes. Use only photos the subject approves and credit the photographer.
  • Double-check names, preferred names, pronouns, titles, spellings, start dates, positions, and results.
  • Avoid negative claims about other people unless the subject agrees and including them is essential.
  • Suggested review: Let the subject fact-check the draft and quotes. They may correct inaccuracies; the guide suggests that they do not rewrite the writer's prose. Confirm this review practice with the section editor.

Process and submission

  1. Get the subject and story angle approved by the section editor.
  2. Secure consent and schedule the interview.
  3. Share the core theme and outline with the editor before drafting.
  4. Draft the profile, verify facts, and follow the editor's revisions. Where agreed, have the subject fact-check facts and quotes.
  5. Submit one to two days before the planned posting date to allow time for review.
  6. Upload the final text, approved photos, writer/layout/photographer credits, and hashtags to the designated SCHEMA Google Drive folder. Notify the editor after uploading and after posting.
  7. For Facebook, prepare subheadings in bold Unicode characters in advance because Facebook does not support bold text formatting.

Writer's checklist

  • The subject is eligible under the section's rules and has consented, including to any sensitive details.
  • One clear theme runs through the piece; the story goes beyond the subject's title or achievements.
  • The opening uses a strong quote or image, and the piece includes a specific scene and a meaningful reflection or lesson.
  • Quotes are verbatim, framed by narration, and presented with the subject's comfort in mind.
  • Names, titles, dates, and results are verified; photos are approved and credited.
  • The ending offers a takeaway for the reader and, where possible, echoes the opening image.
  • Titles, subheadings, credits, hashtags, and format-specific requirements are ready.
  • The finished work is submitted one to two days before posting.
